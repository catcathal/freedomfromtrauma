The heart of this practice: simple principles that point toward real freedom.

An ancient pathway

Letting go is an ancient pathway toward inner freedom — a natural ease that comes when we stop treating “my story,” “my pain,” and “my identity” as the whole of who we are.

When we stop feeding the habit of holding on, the heart gradually returns to ease. What remains is not emptiness, but clarity, peace, and a quiet freedom that does not depend on circumstances.

Not passive observation

Letting go is not a passive observer mode of being. That is a common misunderstanding of the practice.

True practice is active: we strengthen what is clear and kind, and we put in the effort to release the grip of three common habits — craving, resistance, and false identification.

Remembering...

So what do I let go of?

The old habitual pathway — the automatic grip of wanting, resisting, and identifying. Every time a difficult feeling rises, the old pathway says: hold tighter, push away, or become the story. Letting go is choosing a different path: notice, allow, release the wanting, and rest.

The old habitual pathway

The old pathway is familiar. Something arises — fear, anger, craving, shame — and almost without noticing we tighten around it. We feed it with thought, justify it, act it out, or bury it. That cycle is powered by craving (I must have / I must keep), resistance (I must push away / I must fight), and false identification (this story is who I am).

Each time we follow that pathway, it deepens. The practice of letting go interrupts it. We meet what is here without adding another layer of grasping or resistance. Over time the old pathway loses its force, and a freer way of being becomes natural.

It’s about the choice

At the heart of the path is the choice you make — the inner resolve to let go of following the desires that bring dissatisfaction and unhappiness, and that take you out of alignment with Reality. That resolve, renewed again and again, is the practice.

Remembering...

Nothing to gain

There is nothing to gain, nothing to attain, and no one more to become than what you already are. The practice is to settle totally into what you are, and to let go of the drive our culture instills to be something more: the conditioned belief of “I’m not good enough.” Then the natural state of your real nature can be realized.

Whatever you feel stuck on, surrender it

The practice is simple in description and profound in effect. You do not have to force change, argue with the feeling, or wait until you understand everything. You only have to be willing to let it go.

Whatever arises — fear, anger, grief, shame, the need to be right, the hunger for control — can be met, felt, and released. Each time you do this, the grip of the small self loosens a little more.

Remembering...

Not meant to overwhelm

Practice is not supposed to overwhelm you. But there are periods when one should be more committed and resolved, so you can move up a few notches.

The four principles of letting go

A practical way to work with any difficult feeling:

  1. Notice. Become aware of the feeling as it is. Name it simply — anger, fear, hurt, shame — without story or judgment.
  2. Allow. Let the sensation be present in the body. Do not suppress it, act it out, or try to fix it. Give it permission to exist exactly as it is.
  3. Surrender the wanting. Under every stuck feeling is a wanting: for approval, for control, for security, for things to be different. Breathe out and release that demand. You can let go of the need without abandoning yourself.
  4. Rest. Be still. Allow the wave to crest and pass. Do not feed it with more thought or resistance. Rest in the openness that remains.

Go slowly. If any practice feels overwhelming, stop, open your eyes, and orient to the room.

Remembering...

Where the real path begins

The real spiritual path begins when one can maintain the desire to be aligned with Reality, regardless of outside conditions. At first, outside conditions may bring about maturity, stability, and confidence. But the real thing is when that confidence becomes so affirmed that the outside world is no longer feared, because the value of the truth overrides the conditioned value system of conventions and worldly perspectives.

Letting go: common questions

What is letting go?

Letting go is an ancient pathway toward inner freedom. It means releasing the grip of three habits: craving, resistance and false identification (treating “my story” or “my pain” as the whole of who you are). When we stop feeding the habit of holding on, the heart gradually returns to ease.

How do I let go of a difficult feeling?

Use the four principles of letting go. Notice the feeling and name it simply. Allow the sensation to be present in the body. Surrender the wanting underneath it, such as the need for approval, control or security. Then rest, and let the wave crest and pass.

Is letting go the same as suppressing my feelings or giving up?

No. Letting go is not suppression and it is not a passive observer mode. It is an active practice: you feel what is here, strengthen what is clear and kind, and release the grip of craving, resistance and false identification.

What exactly am I letting go of?

The old habitual pathway: the automatic grip of wanting, resisting and identifying. When fear, anger, grief or shame arises, that pathway says hold tighter, push away, or become the story. Letting go is choosing a different path, again and again.

How often should I practise letting go?

Practice is not meant to overwhelm you. Keep it gentle and steady, and renew the choice to let go whenever something arises. There are periods when you may be more committed and resolved, and that is fine.

Letting go lightens the load, in the body and in the mind. As the grip of a separate self loosens, tension melts, dissolves and fades away, and both settle into the natural flow of Reality, at ease with what is.

The warm reds are tension held in the body — the residue of craving, resistance, and old stories. As you let go, they cool into calm blues, and the body softens and breathes freely again.

Hey… I have a body!

As you begin to sit quietly and step back from daily noise, you may become aware of your body. After some time, we become aware of tensions. These tensions take on many forms: in the breathing, a density or heaviness in the chest, clenching of the jaw, a "dissociation" feeling or alienation within the body, a contraction between the eyes, and so on.

How body work helps

Trauma and old reactivity are not only a story held in the mind. They are also held in the body, as chronic tension, bracing, a tight chest, a locked jaw, a stomach that never fully settles. Recovery research on trauma has shown that the body keeps a record of what happened, even long after the mind has moved on or tried to.

In the wild, animals discharge the intense energy of a threat through trembling and movement once danger passes, and then return to ease. Humans often interrupt that natural discharge, so the activation stays trapped in the nervous system. Approaches drawing on this understanding, such as Somatic Experiencing, work by gently helping that stored, thwarted energy complete its release, rather than only by talking or thinking it through.

As the practice of letting go deepens, and as old held patterns continue to surface and move through, the body can shift into a felt sense of safety and begin to relax on its own. Sometimes this relaxing looks calm and quiet. Sometimes it doesn't: shaking, involuntary sounds, or a sudden jolt can arise as the body's own built-in way of unwinding what it has been holding, the same natural mechanism seen in animals that instinctively shake after a threat has passed.

Body work, practiced directly and consistently, gives all of this somewhere to go. Rather than staying stuck as blockages and dense, heavy sensation, energy that moves through the body is free to circulate again, evenly and easily. Body work is a complete path in its own right.

Ways people move stuck energy

These are some of the most common, well-tested ways people work with the body directly to release tension and support a freer flow of energy. Any one of them, practiced gently and regularly, is enough on its own.

Keep in mind: these practices can be intricate and take years of development — like Kriya Yoga, which is considered a path in itself. They are best learned under an experienced teacher. There are many nuances we miss by learning only online. It is wise to stay open-minded and find out what works by getting involved in real-life programs — visiting the center, the program, or the community.

YOGA NIDRA

A guided lying-down practice that scans and relaxes the body into a state of deep rest.

Simply stop suppressing what is real by being honest, starting with yourself. Holding back a truth takes energy; letting it be acknowledged releases it.
